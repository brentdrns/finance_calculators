The Galloway Method: Does Running Slower Actually Make You Faster?

Jeff Galloway has been promoting planned walk breaks since the 1970s — and a lot of recreational runners have run faster marathons using them. Here's what the science actually shows and how to pick the right intervals.

November 2, 2026

The Counterintuitive Claim

The idea sounds wrong on its face: take walk breaks during a race, and you'll finish faster than if you run the whole thing. Yet Jeff Galloway, former Olympic marathoner and coach to hundreds of thousands of recreational runners, has been making exactly this argument for over four decades. And the data, at least for slower recreational runners, largely bears him out.

The mechanism isn't magic. Run/walk intervals work by delaying the accumulation of fatigue in the legs — specifically in the muscle fibers and connective tissue that sustain repeated impact loading over 26 miles. Walk breaks give those fibers a brief recovery window. Done consistently from the start (not reactively when you're already struggling), they allow runners to maintain their running pace throughout the race rather than fading dramatically in the back half.

What the Research Shows

The evidence for run/walk intervals is strongest in the middle-of-the-pack. A 2014 study in the Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport found that recreational marathon runners using planned walk breaks finished in similar or faster times compared to continuous runners of equivalent fitness — primarily because continuous runners slowed significantly in miles 18–26 while walk/run runners maintained more consistent pacing.

The benefit diminishes as pace improves. Elite runners and sub-3-hour marathoners generally do not benefit from walk breaks because their physiology is efficient enough to sustain race pace without the fatigue accumulation that breaks prevent. For runners aiming for 3:30+, the evidence is considerably more favorable.

Injury rate is a secondary benefit with reasonable support. Walk breaks reduce the cumulative impact load per mile, which is the primary driver of overuse injuries like stress fractures, IT band syndrome, and plantar fasciitis. Several studies have found lower injury rates in Galloway-trained runners compared to control groups, though confounding factors (like the Galloway method's emphasis on slower training paces overall) complicate the attribution.

The Math Behind the Method

The effective pace of a run/walk strategy is determined by the ratio of run time to walk time and the paces at each effort:

If you run at 9:00/mile and walk at 15:00/mile with a 4-minute run / 1-minute walk cycle, your effective pace is approximately 10:00/mile. Over a marathon that's a finish time of about 4:22 — about 22 minutes slower than running at 9:00/mile the whole way. But if your continuous running pace fades to 10:30/mile in the back half (common for recreational runners), the run/walk strategy wins.

The break-even point: Run/walk intervals beat continuous running when the pace you maintain with continuous effort in the second half is slower than your run/walk effective pace. For most recreational runners targeting 4:00–5:30 marathons, this crossover point is real — especially if their long run training didn't exceed 18–20 miles or their weekly mileage was under 40 miles per week.

Choosing Your Intervals

Galloway's general guidelines suggest run/walk ratios based on current mile pace:

Current mile paceRun intervalWalk interval
Under 8:00/mile6–8 min30–45 sec
8:00–10:00/mile3–5 min1 min
10:00–12:00/mile2–3 min1 min
12:00–14:00/mile1–2 min1 min
Over 14:00/mile30–60 sec30–60 sec

These are starting points, not rules. The key principle is consistency: take your walk breaks before you feel like you need them. Starting walk breaks when fatigue is already setting in defeats the purpose. Galloway emphasizes beginning walk breaks from the first mile, even when everything feels easy. This is counterintuitive and requires discipline, but it's where most of the benefit accumulates.

Who It Works Best For

  • First-time marathoners who haven't built the aerobic base to sustain race pace for 26 miles without significant fade.
  • Runners over 50, who generally need more recovery time between hard efforts and who often find the reduced impact load meaningfully reduces injury risk during training.
  • High-mileage-limited runners training at under 40 miles per week, whose long runs top out at 18–20 miles — the point at which glycogen depletion and muscular fatigue most reliably cause pace collapse.
  • Ultra-distance runners, where walk breaks are a universal feature of the field at virtually every pace level. The math simply demands it at 50K and beyond.

If you're training for a sub-3:30 marathon with 50+ miles per week and long runs of 22+ miles, continuous running likely serves you better. But for the majority of recreational runners — the 4:00–5:30 cohort that makes up the bulk of marathon fields — the Galloway method deserves serious consideration.

Using It in Training, Not Just Racing

The method also translates into training. Galloway advocates using run/walk ratios on all long runs and easy runs throughout the training cycle, not just on race day. The argument is twofold: it reduces cumulative training injury risk, and it means race day pacing matches what your body has been doing — eliminating the performance unpredictability of trying walk breaks for the first time in a race.
