How Hills Affect Running Pace, Grade by Grade

Every runner knows hills slow you down. Fewer know by how much, or that the downhill on the other side never fully pays you back. Here's what the research says each percent of grade costs, where the downhill benefit runs out, and how to use that to pace a hilly race.

November 6, 2026

The Short Answer

On typical road grades, each 1% of uphill costs roughly 3% of pace at the same effort. A 5% climb makes an 8:00/mile runner about 15% slower, or around 9:12. The same 5% downhill gives back only about 7%, or 7:25.

The downhill benefit grows until about −9%, where it tops out at roughly 9–12% faster than flat. By about −18% you're back to flat pace, and anything steeper is slower than running on level ground. Because climbs take more than descents return, a course that goes up and comes back down is always slower than a flat one.

What the Lab Says: The Energy Cost of Running on a Slope

The most-cited measurement of running on gradients comes from Minetti and colleagues (2002, Journal of Applied Physiology). They put 10 elite mountain runners on a treadmill tilted anywhere from −45% to +45% and measured how much energy each meter of running cost.

On level ground the cost was 3.40 J per kg per meter, regardless of speed. At +10% it rose to 5.77, about 70% more. Downhill it fell to 1.93 at −10% and bottomed out at 1.73 at −20%, roughly half the level cost. Below that it climbed again, reaching 3.92 at −45%, more than running on the flat. A 2017 review in Sports Medicine by Vernillo and colleagues sums up the same picture: the energy cost of running rises steadily with uphill slope, falls on downhills until about −20%, then rises again.

The physics explains the shape. Going up, your muscles do extra work lifting your body against gravity. Going gently down, gravity does some of that work for you. On steep descents your legs spend more and more effort braking, which is work too, done by muscles lengthening under load (eccentric contractions).

There's a catch for runners. Energy cost isn't the same thing as speed. Minetti's team noted that the downhill speeds their numbers predicted were well above what athletes actually run in downhill mountain races. Gravity may make a −20% slope metabolically cheap, but nobody can run it twice as fast as the flat. Leg turnover, footing, stability and the pounding all get in the way first.

What Real Runners Do: Strava's Grade Adjusted Pace

Strava's original Grade Adjusted Pace (GAP) was built on the Minetti curve and ran into exactly that problem. In 2017, Strava engineer Drew Robb rebuilt the model from real-world data: about 6 million runs from 240,000 athletes that had both elevation change and heart rate. Instead of energy cost, the new model asks a simpler question: what pace on flat ground would produce the same heart rate?

The results:

  • Uphill, the lab and the real world mostly agree. Robb describes the two curves as nearly parallel, with the heart-rate model shifted by about 2 points of grade (slightly gentler).
  • Downhill, they disagree a lot. The old lab-based model put the biggest benefit at about −18%, at half the flat-pace cost, which meant running up to twice as fast. The heart-rate model puts the biggest benefit at −9%, worth about 12%, and by −18% the adjustment is back to zero.

That lines up with what most runners feel. Robb notes that runners Strava surveyed didn't think any downhill was worth more than about a 10% speed boost.

Why Downhills Give Back Less Than Uphills Take

Studies differ on the exact numbers, but they all agree on the asymmetry. The reasons stack up:

  • Braking. Past a gentle grade, more of each stride goes into controlling your descent instead of moving you forward.
  • Mechanical limits. To go faster downhill you need longer strides or a faster turnover. In a field study of hill running, Townshend, Worringham and Stewart (2010, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise) found that runners changed speed on hills almost entirely through stride length (20.5% shorter uphill, 16.2% longer downhill) while step rate stayed nearly constant. Stride length can only stretch so far before it turns into overstriding.
  • Muscle damage. Eccentric braking is what leaves your quads sore for days after a downhill race. It doesn't cost much oxygen at the time, but you pay for it later in the race.
  • Footing and confidence. Most people simply won't let go on a steep road, and on trails they shouldn't.

The practical consequence: hills never cancel out. Run one mile up a 5% grade and one mile back down at an 8:00/mile effort, and the model below gives 9:12 + 7:25 = 16:37, which is 37 seconds slower than two flat miles. Even gentle 2% rollers cost about 1% overall. That's why an out-and-back or loop course with lots of climbing is slower than a flat course with the same start and finish.

The Hill Model Our Calculators Use

The Race Pace Planner turns grade into pace with a simple multiplier on your flat-ground pace. With grade G in percent:

Uphill, up to 5%:  1 + 0.028·G + 0.0004·G²
Uphill, 9% and steeper:  Minetti’s energy-cost curve at (G − 2)%, with a smooth blend from 5% to 9%
Downhill (x = −G):  1 − 0.020·x + 0.0011·x²

On a road-race climb that's about 2.8% slower per 1% of grade. Past about 7% the cost rises much faster. Steep climbs follow the lab energy-cost curve, shifted 2 points of grade gentler to match what Strava saw in real runners' heart rates: about 51% slower at 10% and 89% slower at 15%. On a gentle descent it's about 2% faster per 1%, but the benefit flattens out. It peaks at about −9% (9% faster), is back to flat pace at about −18%, and steeper descents are slower than flat, as they are in real life.

These are estimates for a typical runner, not laws. How they compare with the research:

  • Downhill, the model is a little more conservative than Strava's heart-rate curve: a 9% peak benefit instead of about 12%, at the same −9% grade, and the same return to flat pace near −18%. Most runners don't get the full downhill benefit in a race, so we'd rather not promise it.
  • Uphill, on typical road-race grades (up to about 6%) it lands in the same range as Strava's real-world curve. On steeper climbs it follows the lab curve with Strava's 2-point real-world offset. One thing it doesn't model is power-hiking: on very steep trail climbs, many runners hike, which costs less energy than running at the same slow speed. On those stretches, read the split as a target effort, not a requirement to keep running.
  • Individuals vary. Lighter runners, strong climbers and skilled descenders will beat these numbers on their favorite side of the hill. Runners who train only on flat ground will usually lose more.

The hill effort setting

The table above shows even effort: the full multiplier, the pace that would feel the same as your flat pace. The planner has a hill effort slider so you can choose how closely to follow it. How to Pace a Hilly Race

Run by effort, not by pace

Holding your flat pace up a hill is a much bigger effort than it looks. Running 8:00/mile up a 5% grade takes about the same effort as running 6:57/mile on flat ground. Do that on every climb of a marathon and you'll be paying for it by the late miles. Townshend's runners show the natural tendency. Left to pace themselves on a hilly course, they ran 23% slower uphill and 13.8% faster downhill than on the flat, yet their oxygen use still averaged about 100% of their ventilatory threshold on the climbs, against 89% on the level and 79% on the descents. Even experienced runners overspend on the climbs. The runners in that study who let their pace vary more with the gradient kept their oxygen use steadier.

You can't bank time on hills

Pushing a climb to "stay on pace" and planning to "get it back on the downhill" doesn't work for two reasons. The seconds you save uphill cost more effort than the same seconds on the flat. And the downhill can't refund them: past about −9%, pushing harder buys almost nothing, and it costs you quad damage you'll feel at mile 22. The same logic applies to the early-race version of banking time. The VDOT tables assume even pacing for a reason.

On climbs

  • Shorten your stride, keep your cadence and hold the effort and breathing you'd have on the flat. Accept the slower split; it's built into a good plan.
  • Run through the top. In Townshend's study, speed on the flat stayed affected for about 78 seconds after a climb, versus about 24 seconds after a descent. The authors concluded that getting back up to speed on the flat sections after hills is where runners can save the most time.

On descents

  • On gentle grades (roughly 2–6%), let gravity work. Lean slightly forward from the ankles, keep your feet under you and let your turnover come up. That's where most of the free speed is.
  • On steep descents, stay controlled. Past about −10%, extra speed is small and the braking cost is large. Save your legs for the rest of the race.

Example: Boston's Newton Hills

Boston shows both sides of the hill problem. The course drops roughly 130–140 m (about 450 ft) from Hopkinton to Boston, about three times the 1 m per km (42 m over a marathon) that World Athletics allows for a course to count for records. That doesn't make it easy. Most of the drop comes in the first few miles, where it's tempting to run fast and where the eccentric pounding starts. Then the Newton hills come between about miles 16 and 21, ending with Heartbreak Hill between miles 20 and 21. In the profile the Race Pace Planner uses, that mile alone climbs about 28 m (around 90 ft).

For a runner whose flat-effort pace is 8:00/mile, a 4% section of Heartbreak works out to about 8:57/mile at full even effort, or about 8:34 at the planner's default 60% hill effort. Planned in advance, that's just a split on the plan. If it catches you by surprise, you'll try to hold pace and blow up on the climb. How the Race Predictor Accounts for Elevation

The Race Time Predictor uses the same principle at the whole-course level. Instead of working grade by grade, it uses a course's total elevation gain and loss. First it converts your PR to a flat-equivalent time using your PR course's elevation. From that it works out your VDOT, then applies your target course's elevation: climbing adds time, and descending gives back only part of it, the same asymmetry as above. The penalty scales with fitness, so faster runners lose fewer seconds per 100 ft of climbing, partly because they spend less time on each hill. For the 49 marathons with a measured course profile, it also builds a race-day pace plan with hill-adjusted splits using the Race Pace Planner.

The same thinking applies in training. On hilly easy runs, let your pace drift on the climbs and keep the effort easy. Run by feel or heart rate, as covered in Zone 2 training and why most runners train too fast. Your training paces are flat-ground numbers.
