Zone 2 Training: What It Is, Why Everyone's Talking About It

Zone 2 went from a niche coaching concept to mainstream fitness advice thanks to longevity researchers and popular podcasts. Most of what's said about it is correct — but the "how to find it" part is where things go wrong.

November 2, 2026

Why Zone 2 Became a Buzzword

Until a few years ago, "Zone 2" was primarily used by coaches and exercise physiologists. Then it became a centerpiece of longevity medicine — particularly through researchers like Iñigo San Millán and communicators like Peter Attia — who argued that low-intensity aerobic capacity (specifically mitochondrial function) is among the most important physiological metrics for both performance and long-term health. The concept spread rapidly because it validates something counterintuitive: spending more time going slow may be the best long-term investment in your cardiovascular health.

The core claim holds up. Zone 2 training is genuinely one of the most important components of any endurance athlete's plan, and it's chronically underdone by recreational runners. The controversy, such as it is, is mostly about definitions: "Zone 2" means different things depending on which zone system you're using, which creates real confusion about what pace or HR to target.

The Physiology: Why Low Intensity Builds Big Engines

At low aerobic intensity, the primary fuel source is fat, and the primary mechanism for adaptation is mitochondrial biogenesis — the creation of new mitochondria within muscle cells, particularly slow-twitch oxidative fibers. Mitochondria are the organelles that convert fuel (glucose and fatty acids) into ATP, the energy currency of muscle contraction. More mitochondria per cell means more energy production capacity, more efficient fat burning, and better lactate clearance.

This is significant for runners because:

  • A larger, denser mitochondrial network allows you to run faster while staying aerobic — you can generate more energy without triggering lactate accumulation.
  • Fat oxidation capacity determines how long you can sustain marathon-pace effort without depleting glycogen stores. Elite marathoners oxidize fat at rates recreational runners cannot, which is a large part of why they don't hit the wall.
  • Lactate clearance capacity — the ability to process lactate as it accumulates — is closely linked to mitochondrial density in aerobic muscle fibers. A well-developed aerobic base raises your lactate threshold, which effectively moves all your heart rate zones upward.

The Zone Confusion Problem

Here's where many runners get tripped up: different zone systems define "Zone 2" differently. In a 5-zone system based on max HR percentages (the most common in consumer fitness apps), Zone 2 might be defined as 60–70% of max HR. In a 3-zone physiological model, Zone 2 is a moderate intensity between the first and second lactate thresholds — which corresponds roughly to 85–94% of LTHR in the Friel system, closer to Zone 3.

When longevity researchers and coaches like Iñigo San Millán talk about Zone 2, they're almost always referring to the physiological Zone 2 — the highest intensity at which you're primarily oxidizing fat and producing minimal lactate. This corresponds to approximately Zone 1–2 in the Friel/LTHR system (below 89% of LTHR) and is often around 70–80% of max HR in percent-of-max systems. It is not the same as Zone 2 in a 5-zone percent-of-max system.

The simplest field test: at true Zone 2, you should be able to maintain a conversation in full, comfortable sentences — not labored phrases. If you have to pause for breath between sentences, you've drifted above Zone 2. A useful secondary indicator is nasal breathing: many coaches define Zone 2 as the highest intensity at which you can breathe entirely through your nose without discomfort.

Zone 2 in Practice for Runners

For most recreational runners, Zone 2 running is their easy and moderate easy runs — the vast majority of their weekly volume. The practical prescription is straightforward: if it feels easy enough to be slightly embarrassing, it's probably right. The problem is that most runners drift above Zone 2 on their "easy" days, accumulating fatigue and missing the mitochondrial adaptation that Zone 2 specifically provides.

A few practical guidelines:

  • Use LTHR-based zones (from the Heart Rate Zone Calculator) to define the ceiling of Zone 2 precisely. It's below 89% of your LTHR.
  • On hot or hilly routes, pace-based Zone 2 targets will fail — heart rate will climb above Zone 2 at a pace that felt easy on a flat cool day. Run by HR, not pace, when conditions change.
  • Zone 2 adaptation is a slow burn. The benefits accumulate over months of consistent volume, not weeks. Impatience is the main reason runners abandon it before the adaptations show up.
  • Volume matters more than session length. Four 45-minute Zone 2 runs do more than one 3-hour run — because mitochondrial biogenesis is triggered by frequency of aerobic stimulus, not just duration.

How Much Zone 2 Is Enough?

Elite endurance athletes may accumulate 10–15 hours per week at Zone 2. Recreational runners typically have 6–10 hours per week of total training time. The guideline most coaches use: Zone 2 should represent at least 80% of total training volume, which for most recreational runners means 4–7 hours per week of genuinely low-intensity running. If your total weekly mileage is 35 miles, roughly 28 of those miles should be at or below Zone 2 intensity.
